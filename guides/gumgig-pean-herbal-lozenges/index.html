Throat lozenges

Gumgig Pean lozenges: what is in them and how to take them

The licorice and platycodon throat lozenge from Thailand: what the name means, the four herbs on the box, how to take it and who should go easy.

By Dan Ler, Siam Supply Co.  ·  Published 6 November 2026

Gumgig Pean is a herbal throat lozenge made in Thailand from licorice, platycodon (balloon flower root), tea leaf and mint. The box says it is used as an expectorant and for the relief of throat irritation, and that you let one tablet dissolve slowly in the mouth, three to four times a day. It comes in a small yellow box of 20 tablets.

It is one of those products with a loyal, quiet following. People who use it tend to ask for it by name, often because a parent or grandparent kept a box in the drawer. This guide explains what the name means, what is in it, how to take it, and who should go easy on it.

What the name means

The Chinese name on the box, 甘桔喉糖片, is the clearest clue to what it is. Read character by character:

  • 甘 (gan) is from 甘草, licorice root.
  • 桔 (jie) is from 桔梗, platycodon root, also called balloon flower.
  • 喉糖片 (hou tang pian) means throat sweet tablets: a lozenge.

So the name is simply "licorice and platycodon throat lozenges". "Gumgig Pean" is the brand's own romanisation of the same name, with "Pean" standing for pian, tablet. Licorice and platycodon are a classic pairing in Chinese herbal throat remedies, which is why the name leads with them.

What is in a Gumgig Pean lozenge

The box lists four main ingredients, in Chinese and by their Latin names:

  • Licorice root (甘草, Glycyrrhiza glabra), which gives the sweetness and the soft, coating feel.
  • Platycodon root (桔梗, Platycodon grandiflorum), the balloon flower root, traditionally used in Chinese medicine for cough and phlegm.
  • Tea leaf (茶葉), which adds a slightly bitter, dry edge.
  • Mint (薄荷, Mentha arvensis), the field mint that gives the cool finish.

The taste is the giveaway: sharp and herbal rather than sugary, with a licorice sweetness underneath and a mint finish. If you are used to fruit-flavoured cough sweets, it will taste like medicine. That is the point for the people who buy it.

How to take it

The directions on the box are short. Let one tablet dissolve in the mouth without chewing, three to four times a day as needed. Dissolving slowly matters: the lozenge works by keeping the throat coated and moist for as long as it lasts, and chewing it ends that in seconds.

A sore throat from talking all day, air-conditioning or a tickly cough is what most people take it for. If a sore throat comes with a high fever, lasts more than a few days, or makes it hard to swallow, see a doctor rather than working through another box.

Who should go easy on it

Licorice is the ingredient to know about. Licorice root contains a compound called glycyrrhizin, and the US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health notes that it can cause serious effects such as an irregular heartbeat, especially in large amounts or over long periods. It flags people with high blood pressure, heart or kidney conditions, and people who eat a lot of salt, as sensitive even to smaller amounts, and it calls large amounts of licorice in pregnancy unsafe.

A few lozenges for a sore throat is a very different exposure from eating licorice every day. But if you have high blood pressure or a heart or kidney condition, ask your doctor or pharmacist before using a licorice lozenge regularly, and stick to the dose on the box. Lozenges are also a choking risk for small children, so keep them for older children and adults.

The box lists licorice root (Glycyrrhiza glabra), platycodon root (Platycodon grandiflorum), tea leaf and mint (Mentha arvensis).

The box says one tablet at a time, dissolved slowly in the mouth without chewing, three to four times a day.

Licorice and platycodon throat lozenges: 甘 from licorice root (甘草), 桔 from platycodon root (桔梗), and 喉糖片 meaning throat lozenge tablets.
